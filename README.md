# Claybound

**Kneadable clay is no longer a fourth-chapter mechanic, and it is no longer a speed bump either.** Each of the first three chapters stops mid-route, pushes the rest of the chapter bodily to the right and builds a clay section into the space that opens up — `makeRoom()` in `dist/route-authoring.js` does the shifting, so the chapter above each call is still the route exactly as it was authored. The canyon's **Sandwright's Pocket** (x124–160) is one free formable mass — the Clay Lab's `form` rule, promoted — sitting on a sandstone shelf that fills the chasm: it rests as a spire flush with the dock's end, a unit out of a jump's reach and a wall to walk into, a skim of clay over the pit behind it, and a lump against the far cliff cresting above the landing. The shelf is sown with spikes half a unit under the clay's base, so **clay is ground and sand scraped bare is not** — any clay at all is safe, and the mass never thins past a third of a unit between its ends. Nothing there can be jumped; the pocket opens only once the clay is worked, and any shape that carries you counts: the station's authored `solution` — grab the spire's crest and lean it into the pit, grab the lump's crest and slump it into a ramp — is one way of several (`tests/clay-sections.mjs` also crosses it by squashing the spire flat from the air, by pulling a pillar up under your own feet and stomp-launching over the lump, and by E-steps up the lump's face, and once by the keyboard alone). That solution is what "shaped" means for a mass with no finished pose: `solveFormStation` in `dist/clay-rules.js` replays the strokes through the real hand from the clump, the routes sweep copies the surface it makes, the playthrough pilot plays the very same strokes as real pointer inputs, and the tests hold the two to be bitwise equal. The pocket's clay does not slump back on its own (`relax:false`), so what you made is still there when you die and respawn at the dock — R softens the whole pocket back, from off the clay — and its shaped share is stiffer than the lab's (`shaped:.24`), so the toast and the fading hand cue arrive only once about as much clay has moved as the full solution moves, never after a dab or one leaned tower. A checkpoint beyond the pocket resumes with the authored solution rebuilt rather than the player's own heap — the save records that the clay was crossed, not its shape — while a save short of it resumes with the clump, however much was worked, so nothing is ever earned by reloading. A keyboard or a pad opens the pocket too: E works whatever clay is ahead into a step you can walk up — pressing it down where it towers over you, lifting it where it lies below, and at the clay's end, with nothing ahead to step onto, lifting the ground underfoot — and it never draws that clay from under your own feet. The Wildwood's **Weaver's Gap** (x192–232) draws a bough stub out over a tear in the canopy, then asks for a mushroom bounce onto a mound far too tall to land on — so it has to be squashed flat from across the gap first. Ember Caverns' **Kiln** (x57–93) has a clay tower sealing the way under a rock lintel: stand on it and press, and it squats away from the rock into the kiln floor with you still on it; under the lintel a plug filling the tunnel from floor to rock is spread flat and walked across. Two rules hold for every piece of clay in every chapter, and `tests/clay-sections.mjs` enforces both: **one input works one piece** — no gesture ever moves a second piece of clay — and **clay keeps its volume**, so whatever gets wider gets lower (a ramp counts its wedge, a slab its thickness) within ten per cent — at both ends and at every point in between, because while clay is being worked its height is solved from the blended volume rather than blended on its own, and a half-pressed piece no longer swells to twice its clay; the formable mass keeps its volume exactly, whatever it is dragged into. The Hanging Quarter was brought into line too: its first ramp is pulled from a lump big enough to make it, the laundry wall is one piece pulled over into one slope instead of three slices moving together, and the belfry plug is pressed down into the gap at full depth instead of being squashed thin. For each of the new pieces the same test walks the bypass with only that piece unworked and insists it is impossible, checks the unworked top sits out of reach of the best jump, mushroom bounce or stomp-bounce before it (or is capped by rock — the Kiln tower alone is meant to be climbed onto; for the pocket's mass the top is the first place on its rest surface that is neither a face to slide down nor sand to die on), checks that softening finished clay underfoot opens no way past the next piece, and for the pocket walks a real-input pilot from dock to landing over the solved surface and over the clump — so none of the new clay can be jumped past, and two route links are never asked to vouch for the middle of one mass. All four gestures — down, right, out and the free hand's up — are taught before the Hanging Quarter. Layout versions move to canyon 9, forest 10, cave 10 and city 6, which retires old checkpoints in all four chapters.

**The Brittle Canopy has a horizon of its own.** Its crumbling boughs used to run against nothing but haze; the supplied waterfall now stands behind them, rising at the right edge as the first bough is reached and standing squarely ahead by the last. It is one authored landmark rather than another tiled crown, so it lives in a parallax layer of its own: a span wider than any chapter means the wrap that keeps the repeating layers full can never bring a second copy into view, and that layer's own drift is what gives the vista a passage-sized life. Its place is read off the passage rather than written as a world coordinate — the passage carries a `backdrop` field in `dist/routes/forest.js`, and both offsets are authored net of the layer's horizontal scroll and vertical follow, so re-cutting the chapter carries the vista along with it. Depth sits it between the two crown layers, where the hazy crowns and the distant ropeways pass behind the cliff while the near trunks still sweep in front of it, and at that distance the fog leaves the rock and the water their colour. It is sunk far enough that the rock runs off the bottom of the frame instead of ending in mid-air. The GLB ships at 0.96 MB instead of 6.52 MB, geometry and UVs copied through unchanged and hash-verified (`scripts/prepare-forest-assets.py`). Composition was matched to the supplied target with live WebGL captures along the passage (`scripts/review-canopy.cjs`, renders in `docs/forest/brittle-canopy/`); scene checks cover the single copy, the depth and drift ordering, that the falls are framed throughout their passage and gone from the rest of the chapter, and that their base stays below the bottom edge from the lowest bough the passage is seen from.

**The workshop can dress a chapter now, not just lay out its route.** A **Decorate** toggle in the tool bar switches the level editor to the chapter's scenery layer, edited with the tools that were already there: tap a prop to select it, drag to move, drag either round handle to resize it about its own centre, and Add offers a palette of shapes rather than platforms. Undo, redo, duplicate, delete, the nudge keys, snapping, Browse and Test all behave as they do for a deck. While it is open the platforms, beads, enemies and hazards stay drawn faintly but cannot be selected, so a tap never has to choose between a prop and the ground behind it, and the sparse foreground props each chapter grows on its own become visible and hold still — for the rest of editing they are hidden, because a prop that fades out under whatever the cursor is over is a prop nobody can position. Each placement carries a shape, a **Size across** that means the width of the silhouette for a built pebble and the supplied castle alike, a **signed depth** (below zero behind the platforms, above zero in front of them), and a **turn** and **lean** in degrees. The palette is per chapter, because a placement names one of the shapes the game already knows how to build: seven — boulders, pebbles, a rock shelf, a clay pot, a torch, a tree and a cloud — take the chapter's own palette and are offered everywhere, while the canyon adds its arches, summits, cacti, tents and cave mouth, the Wildwood its mushrooms, blooms, crowns, groves and falls, Ember Caverns its crystals, amber mushrooms, moss and grotto rock, and the Hanging Quarter its cottages, laundry, doorways and the skyline castle. **The scenery the chapters already carry is editable too, where it was already data.** Everything reachable is outlined, because a landmark stands two to five units above the deck that owns it and a deck's hit line is a thin band at its surface — so the prop you were looking at could be missed entirely while the bare rock below it was the only way in. Decoration mode now draws a dashed box where each prop actually stands, measured against the built geometry rather than guessed at, and a tap inside it selects the platform that carries it; the deck line still wins ties and stays the precise way in. What has no outline has nothing to select, which is how the cacti, moss and checkpoint trees grown from a platform's own geometry are told apart from the landmarks that are real data — and because dressed decks are sparse (eight across the canyon's 282 units), Browse lists every one by its prop and jumps there. Tapping a platform while decorating opens the landmark it carries and its house/arch/entrance/rest flags — and nothing that would change the route: the deck cannot be dragged, resized, nudged, copied or deleted from that panel, and Browse lists every platform already carrying scenery so an authored landmark can be found by name. The same name is labelled as the chapter actually builds it, because the art differs — `arch` is a stone arch in the caverns and the canyon's camp tent, `sandwheel` a windmill everywhere but the canyon, where it is the eroded basin. Two chapters do not let the name decide and the panel says so instead of offering a choice it cannot keep: Ember Caverns keys seven arrangements off the platform's own id, so there the control is a switch that turns the deck's story piece on or off, and the Hanging Quarter does the same for its laundry courtyard while ignoring a landmark entirely on the two decks that tell their story through props. `story-landmarks.js` now reads that id table from the same place the workshop does, so the two cannot drift apart. What is still out of reach is the dressing computed from a platform's own width and position — the moss caps, the tree on each checkpoint deck, the mushrooms, crystals and torches — and the parallax crowns and skylines, all of which would have to become authored data first. **Decoration is scenery and nothing else** — never a collider, a collectible or a checkpoint, and it never reaches the simulation as anything a player can touch, so there is no way to make a chapter unplayable with it; a draft names a shape rather than carrying geometry, and an unrecognised name is refused on import instead of carried. It streams as scenery too, behind every deck the player is about to land on, and backdrop placements drop out of the shadow pass. A chapter with no decoration keeps exactly the layout version it had, so this list existing retires nobody's checkpoints. Checks run at all three levels: the catalogue's shapes, defaults, palettes, carry-with-platform, inertness in the real simulation and refused placements (`tests/editor.mjs`); the toggle, palette, properties, pointer move and handle resize with live preview, history, playtest return and mode boundaries (`tests/editor-ui.mjs`); and every palette shape built in its own chapter against the real models, with exact placement, measured widths, the shadow budget and streaming in and out without disposing a shared model (`tests/scene.mjs`). Live WebGL appearance of a decorated chapter on a device remains unverified.

The world answers as clay too. A landing dents the slab under it: a damped spring with one overshoot, about one and a half per cent under an ordinary landing and three capped under a stomp, scaled down on a long slab because a long plank flexes less. A stomped creature is pressed into a disc, held flat where the player is already looking, then peeled away — it used to squash to nothing in two tenths of a second, which reads as deleted. Neither moves a collider; the dent is skipped under reduced motion.

One bench is offered rather than shipped. **The Clay Lab** (chapter picker, under the four) is four experiments in what clay does besides hold a shape: a deep block of soft clay that SAGS under your weight and keeps part of the sag, a lump you PACK until it throws you, a row of slabs you SIZE one at a time into a stair, and a whole trough of clay you FORM freely. The fourth, **Fully Formable** (x108–141), has no pose at all: its top is a row of columns over the trough floor (`dist/clay-form.js`, tested on its own and through the real simulation in `tests/clay-form.mjs`), and the pointer is the tool — take hold of any point of the surface and drag, and the clay within a reach follows the hand with a soft falloff, up into a pillar or a step, down into a bowl, sideways into a lean or a bridge; press in from the air above and it dents where the hand enters; tap it and it pokes; hold E and the clay ahead of you is worked into a step you can walk up (pressed down where it towers, lifted where it lies low); stomp it and it craters and throws you straight back up, the way the packed lump does at full but on the first stomp, every time (about six and two-thirds units). Three promises hold every tick: it stays one blob (no column thinner than a third of a unit), it keeps its volume (a pillar digs its own moat and a bowl grows its own rim, nearest first), and it stays usable (no column past 8.5, no face steeper than 4.5 in 1, no corner turned tighter than a 0.6 radius — every edge a stroke would leave sharp is rounded off as it is made — and small strokes smoothed). Weight is a tool here too, lightly and bounded — a stand sinks the boots about a third of a unit and pushes up shoulders, landings and stomps a little more, never past a cap — which is the Sag & Set behaviour inside the one simulation. Collision reads the same columns the mesh does, with two rules a heightfield alone lacks: a face steeper than a walkable slope is a wall — a walker stops at its foot, a jumper who hits it slides down it, and a walker who steps onto one falls — and clay rising into airborne feet lifts them rather than shoving them aside. Left alone (no hand, nobody standing on it) for a few seconds the whole surface slumps slowly back to its authored clump, so a player's ramp or pillar is a temporary solution and the bench is always recoverable; R puts the clump back at once. The slab starts flat and square, a step below the benches, so nothing is shaped for you; the flower perch above is out of a stomp's reach from the slab, so it wants a low pillar pulled up under it and a stomp from its top. Beside it, **A Lump on the Bench** (x141–167) is the same clay sitting free on the bench instead of let into it: a square blob with rounded shoulders whose faces are too steep to climb and whose top is too high to jump to, thinning to nothing at the ends of its footprint (but never past a third of a unit between them, so it is always one lump), with a flower perch so high above it that even a stomp from its top falls short until the lump is drawn up. Lean it into a ramp, spread it flat, pull a step up at its foot or draw it up into a pillar — the bench under it runs on bare on either side. Every piece of violet clay in the game, chapter or lab, is heard being worked: whatever moves it — a drag, E, a tap, boots sinking in, a stomp — raises a `knead` event from the simulation no oftener than every half second while the work goes on (`KNEAD_GAP` in `dist/shaping.js`), and the sound answers each with one of three supplied kneading takes (`dist/assets/clay-knead-1..3.wav`, folded to mono) chosen at random and never the same one twice running; clay settling, slumping or relaxing on its own is silent. `tests/clay-knead.mjs` drives every kind of clay and every way in, and `tests/audio.mjs` the takes. The block is a row of springs across its top (`dist/clay-give.js`, tested on its own in `tests/clay-give.mjs`) that the collider and the mesh both read. It behaves like heavy clay rather than jelly: standing sinks you about a player-height and a third below the shoulders it pushes up and settles without ringing, and whatever you press in — a stand, the trough a walk drags behind you, a stomp crater — it gives back only about a quarter of and keeps the rest, with no wake, no swing and no throw. The flower perch above it belongs to a running jump off the start bench. The lump and the slabs take hands as well as boots: stomp them, drag them (the lump down, a slab up or down), tap them, or hold E, which packs the lump or grows the slab you stand on or face; a lump that has just thrown you ignores the hand that armed it until that hand lets go. Every one carries a `rule`. One of them has left the bench: the formable mass is the canyon's Sandwright's Pocket, with its slump switched off and a stiffer shaped share, and `tests/clay-lab.mjs` asserts that no other rule reaches a chapter; which rules take a hand is listed in `dist/clay-rules.js`, and `tests/clay-lab-hands.mjs` drives the real pointer and key handlers against them.

A pass over loading, download weight, input and audio options. **The loading screen now stays up until the chapter is actually built.** It used to be dismissed the moment the downloads finished, leaving the player looking at an empty sky with a live HUD on it for several seconds while `world.build` welded the chapter on the main thread; the overlay now covers that too, and drops back to its indeterminate sweep — a compositor-driven transform, so the bar keeps moving even while the main thread is blocked. A response with no `Content-Length` no longer parks the bar at zero per cent.

Seven supplied models were about 95% texture, and are repacked with the same method `prepare-drifter.py` and `prepare-tent.py` already used: every map capped at 1024 pixels square, re-encoded as 4:4:4 JPEG, normal maps renormalised, geometry, rigs, animations and UVs copied through byte for byte and hash-verified. The Echo Spitter's crystal alone was 14.66 MB — a 3,045-triangle prop carrying a 4096-pixel roughness map. Together with mono/trimmed effect recordings the download drops from **93 MB to 51 MB** with no visible change; what is left is mostly the five soundtracks, which stream one at a time. `scripts/prepare-textures.py` and `scripts/prepare-effects.py` reproduce both passes. `player.glb` and `enemy.glb` now carry a `shippedSha256` beside their original `sourceSha256`, so the motion data is still pinned to exactly one file.

**Gamepads and haptics are supported.** A standard-mapping controller steers with the left stick or d-pad, jumps on A/Y, stomps on B/X/RT/d-pad-down and pauses on Start; on the title, A or Start begins. Keyboard keeps priority, so a stick resting off centre can never fight a held key. Phone vibration and controller rumble fire from the same event bus the particles use, scaled by impact and rate-limited so a coin run cannot stack into one buzz — `dist/gamepad.js`, `dist/haptics.js`, covered by `tests/gamepad.mjs`. **Music and effects now have independent sliders**, on their own Web Audio buses, so the soundtrack can be silenced without losing the landing and pickup cues; music defaults below effects. Rumble has its own switch.

**The Hanging Quarter is rebuilt as the mastery chapter (layout version 4, which restarts incompatible chapter-four checkpoints; other chapters keep their layouts and progress).** It still owns counterweight beams and paired gondolas, but it no longer introduces a mechanic and then drops it: the canyon's draughts, Wildwood's springs, the caverns' pulse decks, and drifters, claylings, bats and one Echo Spitter all return in new combinations, and the chapter closes on a climax rather than a walk to the bell — the belfry draught, three failing tiles over a flower pocket that doubles as the lower catch, and a clay plug the player presses into the final bridge.

Kneadable clay is no longer confined to the playground. Three stations carry the chapter: a ramp onto the first roof (taught over safe ground), a clay wall pulled over into a slope to reach the laundry roof, and the last span to the sky bell. Finished shapes survive respawns and are stored with the checkpoint, so resuming never drops the player in front of clay they already worked. Tap or drag the clay itself, hold `E`, or stomp clay you can stand on; `R` softens it back. There is no knead panel — input is on the clay, not in a box.

Clay asks for itself, and there is no panel of text anywhere in the loop. Kneadable clay is its own material — violet (`#7a55b5`, caps `#8b68c7`), smoother and glossier than the sculpted world, with a shallower relief and hand-pressed lumps along its sides and base — so it never depends on colour alone to read as different. Standing beside unworked clay sets it breathing: a slow squash under two per cent about its own centre, with its grip mark swelling in time. The collider never moves; the breath is feel, not geometry, and it freezes with the game and holds still under reduced motion (`dist/shaping-views.js`).

Every way a player might touch clay moves it. A drag is the precise control, but a **tap presses it** (three tenths per tap, so four taps finish a piece) and a **stomp works clay of any gesture**, not only press-down clay. Clay that ignores a tap reads as clay that is not interactive at all, which is precisely how a stair wall once looked broken.

The hand is the tutorial layer on top of that: a continuous cream sculpture with an extended index finger, three curled knuckles, a thumb and a rounded wrist. It touches the upper face of the clay and mimes the required stroke — pressing down on the belfry plug, pulling the ramp and the laundry wall to the right. Three rounded dashes lead to a thick, rounded arrow in the same cream. The hand keeps its upright pointing pose during horizontal drags so its silhouette stays readable (`dist/shape-hand.js`). **Teaching is per piece of clay**: clay the player has never finished always shows its cue, clay they have solved before stays quiet, and it returns after a second and a half of hesitation — never while a knead is in progress. Chapter four carries no instruction popup for anything chapters one to three already teach either: by the last chapter it explains only the counterweight beams and the blinking lamp decks, which nothing before it puts into words.

Ember Caverns now uses overlapping custom grotto formations at two scales, irregular rounded columns and ceiling rocks, hanging olive moss, and moss-edged stone ledges. The distant chambers recede into blue haze while amber mushrooms and cyan crystals light the nearer shelves. The scene is built from the two existing supplied models; no additional scenery model is required.

Wildwood has four Spore Puffs using the supplied green mushroom character. They wiggle, release a brief stunning puff, crouch, then leap at the warned position. Control returns before the leap; jumping on the cap defeats them, and a defeated one is pressed flat while the cloud it attacks with escapes all round it and drifts off. Their placement and patrol are editable.

Ember Caverns received a performance pass that leaves its layout, collision, lighting and composition untouched. Static backdrop cells are baked into one mesh per material and block of space, so the chapter submits about a fifth fewer draw calls and carries a fifth fewer scene nodes, with its triangle count unchanged; supplied grotto instances and light fixtures stay separate meshes. Per-frame work that walked every platform's meshes now uses lists gathered once, untouched crumbling decks stop rewriting their fragment transforms, and backdrop light fixtures read the parallax transforms already resolved for them. Level building is faster everywhere: welding sculpted vertices uses a spatial hash instead of a string key per point, and the subdivision that adds silhouette relief no longer allocates vectors per corner, producing byte-identical geometry. The clay shape cache is now bounded by the memory it holds rather than by a shape count, which roughly halves the vertex data a cave session keeps resident and cuts streaming hitches in every chapter. Measurements come from `tests/perf.mjs`; live GPU behaviour on a device remains unverified.

The cave now draws its scenery directly with the playfield, bypassing the offscreen blur/composite path after a phone capture showed only the clear colour. Distance fog separates the layers, with the cave range tightened to 30–98 so the far arches fade more strongly into blue haze while the playfield stays clear. Regression checks inspect the actual render-call boundary and visible cave meshes at five route positions in portrait and landscape, including chapter return and backtracking. These checks validate scene submission, not phone GPU output.

Orange mushroom and blue crystal emission is isolated from the supplied models' existing colour maps; the rock remains dark. Light anchors sit at each glowing cluster, including both shelves of Crystalcap Cavern. Three nearby fixtures use a fixed pool of four unshadowed point lights: the fourth overlaps an outgoing and incoming source over 0.6 seconds, one handoff at a time. A closer-source margin prevents camera jitter from toggling fixtures. Light power stays steady between handoffs; detached or wrapped fixtures fade at their last position. Distant grotto formations retain their emissive maps/surfaces without competing for nearby lights. The two GLBs total 2.11 MB and load only when needed by the cave or its editor. The art changes preserve all routes, collisions, checkpoints and mechanism data. Checks cover continuous ceiling geometry, direct draw coverage, retained source models, fog, camera jitter, reversals, streaming removals, parallax wrapping and the four-light budget; composition is reviewed with offline renders rather than a live GPU capture.

The supplied soundtracks loop in their assigned worlds: **Steps Along the Ridge** in Sunbaked Canyon, **Morning at the Breathing Tree** in Wildwood, **Where Crystals Sing** in Ember Caverns, and **Above the Clay Horizon** on the title screen and in Hanging Quarter. The original stereo MP3s are streamed through one reusable audio element after a tap or key press; only the selected song is loaded. Title-to-Hanging Quarter retains playback position; changing to another chapter starts its song with a short fade-in. Music ducks in quiet passages, fades and pauses with gameplay, and stops immediately when muted or the page loses focus. A failed track falls back to that chapter's synthesized motif without affecting other songs. Effects keep their own routing. Automated checks cover transitions, mute/resume, autoplay rejection and abandoned playback requests; real-device sound balance remains unverified.

Sunbaked Canyon's wind wells breathe under the soundtrack rather than switching on at their edge: a supplied recording, trimmed to a seamless loop (`scripts/prepare-canyon-wind.py` strips the inaudible sub-20 Hz rumble that was most of its energy and resamples to 12 kHz), plays as two detuned, offset voices so a two-second clip does not repeat audibly, fading in over the last few units of approach to an active well and fading out on the way clear. A gust swell on its own timer ebbs the bed between quiet and full over a few irregular seconds at a time, independently of the player's position, so a well standing open still sounds like weather rather than a held tone. Silent for any well waiting on its switch, and off in every other chapter.

All eighteen wooden direction signs are mounted on named platforms. Posts follow platform motion and editor moves, stay inside resized deck edges, and disappear when their supporting bridge or seal is inactive, broken or deleted.

Gameplay messages are limited to essential mechanic hints, short activation feedback and actionable errors. Checkpoints use the flag-hoisting animation. Chapter introductions, incidental passage messages and repeat collectible/respawn toasts are removed; hints dismiss automatically after 6.5 seconds or on leaving their location. Persistent health, collectible counts and bridge timers remain.

The completion screen is a full-screen clay celebration with four biome scenes, live run statistics and personal-best timing.

An in-engine **Level editor** is available from the title screen and pause menu. Shape all four chapters with touch dragging, resize handles, mechanism properties, undo/redo, automatic device-local drafts, JSON backups and real playtesting. See [EDITOR.md](EDITOR.md) for controls and persistence behavior. Edited chapters use separate records; original level data remains available through Restore original.

A four-chapter, mobile-friendly 2.5D platformer. The scene is rendered in Three.js with clay-textured meshes, soft shadows, four distinct environments and the supplied Meshy character with skeletal animation. Gameplay stays on a 2D plane.

## Documentation in this repository

This repository holds the game, its tests and the few operational documents needed to run and extend it: this README, `LOCAL-DEPLOYMENT.md`, `EDITOR.md` and `ASSET_REQUESTS.md` (the dimensions and pivots supplied art has to match).

**Design reviews, playtest write-ups, ideation and "what we should do next" documents do not belong in the repository.** They date instantly, they drift out of step with the code, and nothing in the build reads them. Put that thinking in the pull request description, the issue, or the conversation that produced it — the place where it can be answered — and land only the change itself. A previous playtest report and a ten-point design review were removed for this reason; both remain in git history (before commit `491e500`) if they are ever needed again.

`docs/` is therefore not a writing folder. What is left there is evidence and fixtures that code actually uses: `tests/editor.mjs` and `tests/press-relay-fixture.mjs` load JSON from `docs/cave-layout-canon/` and `docs/forest-canopy-canon/`, and the `scripts/review-*.cjs` capture tools write their output into it. Anything else under `docs/` is a leftover capture and can go.

`tests/layout-audit.mjs` is the authoring companion to this rule: run `node tests/layout-audit.mjs [chapter]` while laying out a route and it reports decks buried inside another deck's solid body, hazard bands that reach a standing surface, crossings outside the measured jump envelope, beads inside hazards, flowers with no ledge under them, and enemies crowding a checkpoint flag.

## Chapters

Each chapter is rebuilt around a small set of connected mechanics and is exactly **30% of the previous extended route's distance**. Five or six passages introduce an idea, develop it, change its context, then combine it in a finale. The obsolete district generator is removed. Coordinates, return paths, secrets and safety shelves are authored explicitly.

| Chapter | Distance | Core arc |
|---|---:|---|
| Sunbaked Canyon | 280.5 units | Learn rope lifts; open a windwell; cross sinking sandstone; lean a clay spire into a bridge and slump a lump into a ramp — or build your own way across; climb around the arch; combine wind, lifts and crumbling ledges. |
| Wildwood | 300.5 units | Mushroom chains; stomp into a root cellar; climb both sides of a living tree; open spore vents; cross a brittle canopy, draw a bough over the tear in it and bounce onto a mound you flattened first, then bloom into the crown. |
| Ember Caverns | 324.5 units | Open passages by climbing and returning; ride a clay tower down into the kiln and flatten the plug in its tunnel; steer a ferry through presses; ride rotating cradles; descend to a sunken relay and return by lift; outwit projectile shooters. |
| Hanging Quarter | 300.75 units | Knead a ramp onto the first roof; weigh down beams to raise permanent ropeways; ride awnings and blinking lamp decks; read a shooter from cover; wake the belfry draught, cross failing tiles and press the last bridge yourself. |

Each chapter has secret flowers (three in the canyon, the caverns' five, two in Wildwood and three in the Hanging Quarter), clay beads, checkpoint flags, a finish bell and replay. Thirty-four checkpoints restore health and preserve collectible IDs, elapsed time, broken seals and permanently activated mechanisms. Older layout checkpoints are invalidated so the player cannot resume inside the redesigned terrain; previous best records remain available in the local save history.

`dist/routes/` contains the four layouts. `dist/route-authoring.js` contains only data helpers, never procedural encounters or distance padding. `ASSET_REQUESTS.md` lists optional replacements for the working clay mechanism props.

## Controls

- **A / D** or **left / right arrows**: move.
- **Space / W / up arrow**: jump; hold for a longer jump.
- **S / down arrow**: stomp while airborne; drop through a thin ledge while standing on it.
- **Escape / P**: pause.
- **F**: toggle native fullscreen. Fullscreen buttons are also available in the title screen, HUD and pause menu.
- **Settings → Play fullscreen**: begin directly in fullscreen; touch devices request landscape where supported.
- Touch devices display an analog thumb joystick alongside jump and stomp buttons. A generous neutral zone and gradual speed curve make small steps easier; drag farther for a full run. The joystick captures one thumb until release, even outside its ring, while the other thumb can jump or stomp. Lifting, interrupted touches, pause, fullscreen and rotation reset it safely.

Flower pickups trigger a 0.5-second upper-body flourish while gameplay, the run timer and the current lower-body animation continue. The arms raise a glowing flower, the supplied `dist/assets/flower-victory.wav` plays over ducked music, and the regular follow camera stays unchanged. Reduced motion simplifies the shine. `tests/flower-celebration.mjs` checks concurrent gameplay and cleanup, and `scripts/review-flower.cjs` captures the actual desktop/portrait celebration.

The simulation runs at a fixed 120 Hz and is independent of rendering. It includes coyote time, jump buffering, variable-height jumps, faster reversals, firm braking, a small apex pause, moving-platform carry, short invulnerability after damage and checkpoint recovery. Resize, focus loss and graphics context loss are handled explicitly. Sound uses the Web Audio API after a player gesture.

## Custom character

The source mesh, rig, colour texture and all 11 supplied clips are preserved in the GLB. `GLTFLoader` and its dependencies are bundled locally with the same Three.js release as the renderer. The character loads once and is reused across all chapters; loading progress and a retry error are shown before play becomes available.

Gameplay uses the newly supplied `Armature|Idle_9|baselayer` as the standard idle. After four seconds of uninterrupted rest, `Idle_03` plays once as a fidget, then blends back to the standard idle. Movement, airborne states and damage interrupt it and reset the timer; pausing freezes the pose and timer. `Walking`, `Running`, `Regular_Jump`, `Jump_Over_Obstacle_2`, `Face_Punch_Reaction_2`, `Knock_Down` and `Skip_Forward` handle the other states. Walking and running blend with analog movement speed. Jump excerpts cover takeoff, held airborne poses, falling, stomping and landing; moving jumps use the obstacle-leap clip. Damage, checkpoint respawns, pause and chapter completion have explicit transitions. The base pose, pushing and slide clips remain in the asset for later mechanics.

`node scripts/prepare-idle.mjs PATH_TO_IDLE_GLB` verifies compatibility with the existing player rig and generates the small animation-only data file. `node scripts/prepare-enemy.mjs` regenerates the enemy's foot corrections from its shipped GLB. Both preserve the original model geometry, texture and animation poses; translations are corrected so game physics owns movement and feet meet the floor. Enemy walk speed follows patrol speed, pause freezes the animation and a stomp produces a brief squash before hiding the defeated enemy.

The model is uniformly scaled to 1.78 world units, faces the travel direction and has a foot-centred gameplay origin. Root translation cannot move the collision body or double the physical jump height. Grounded clips use offline-sampled sole corrections; no vertex scan occurs in the frame loop. The imported metallic/emissive settings are adjusted to a matte clay surface so the original colour texture responds to canyon sunlight, forest light and cave torches. The supplied topology, texture and bone poses are retained.

`tests/player.mjs` parses the shipped GLB and samples every gameplay clip for finite transforms, stable root placement and floor alignment, then exercises the controller with the real game simulation. Its Node texture hook preserves texture references without uploading images to a GPU. Texture decoding is checked separately; these checks do not establish browser or device rendering performance.

## Choosing a character

Settings holds a hidden cast: the original, the Emberleaf wanderer, the Clay wanderer and the Garden helper. The picker stays out of sight until `ß` is typed with the settings panel open, and once found it stays unlocked on that device. The choice applies immediately — the rig is released and the next one attached on the same group the game already drives, so the title scene or a chapter in progress keeps its position, camera and level. Only the chosen character is downloaded; the others cost nothing until they are picked.

Every character is interchangeable rather than merely loadable. Each carries the same nine states under the same names over the same foot-centred origin, with its own offline-sampled sole corrections, so chapters play identically whoever is worn. Each declares its own standing height — the original 1.78 world units, the three supplied characters half again as tall — and the shadow, the flower reach and the daze motes that circle the head all scale with it. Their rigs answer to one vocabulary of joint names, which is what lets the animation states and the two-handed flower celebration address any of them. Only the original is pulled toward the game's orange by the clay shader; the supplied characters were painted in clay colours already and keep their skin exactly as it was made, taking just the clay surface relief.

The three newer files each arrived with a single `Running` cycle on a differently named humanoid rig. `python scripts/prepare-textures.py SOURCE_GLB dist/assets/NAME.glb` repacks their textures to the mobile budget, then `node scripts/prepare-character.mjs NAME` retargets the original character's whole clip set onto the new skeleton and bakes its floor corrections. The original rig rests in an A-pose and the supplied rigs in a T-pose, so what crosses over is each joint's absolute world orientation rather than its rotation away from its own rest — the only constant kept is the roll between the two bone frames once their rest limbs are swung into line. The supplied geometry, skinning and textures are untouched, and the result is written beside the model as `NAME-animation.json` and `NAME-motion.json`.

`tests/characters.mjs` loads every character in the registry and holds all of them to the same bar: matching asset hashes, the complete state vocabulary, the declared height over the foot origin, arms that hang the way the original's do measured against the character's own height, feet on the floor throughout every grounded clip, no root drift or airborne displacement, both hands converging on the flower, the declared colour treatment on every material, and walk/run/leap blending under the real simulation. It then swaps through the whole cast inside one running world and back, asserting that exactly one rig is ever attached and that the newcomer takes over the run mid-stride.

The canyon uses blue sky, warm terracotta foreground, peach atmospheric distance, green supplied cacti and orange pulley details. Its supplied enemy/player models and clay surfaces are retained; the shortened route connects windwells, ropes, sinking sandstone and vertical arch paths. Impact feedback includes dust, skids, a brief hit pause, restrained camera shake and collectible feedback. Reduced-motion preferences disable camera shake and hit pauses and reduce particle counts.
