# Claybound

**The horizon is authored now, not only grown.** A **backdrop** list stands the shapes the workshop already knows in the chapter's parallax layers, so far scenery can be placed, moved, resized, turned and leaned like a prop instead of only existing in code. A piece is authored by **the world x it is centred on** — stored inside its layer as that x times its distance, which is the arithmetic that makes it arrive exactly there when the camera does and drift past either side at the rate its depth implies; the editor draws and drags it through the same expression, written as a drift from its own x so the answer is exact at the anchor rather than a rounding error away. **Distance** is the new property, 0.05 in the far sky to 0.95 almost in the playfield, and depth reaches sixty units back, because the canyon's own skyline stands at fifty-three and the caverns' veil at fifty-nine and a list that stopped at forty-four could not hold what it exists to stand beside. Each distance costs one parallax layer however many pieces share it, an authored piece never tiles, and the whole list is built with its chapter rather than streamed, because at a low distance a piece is in frame for a hundred units of travel either side. It is bounded and counted separately from decoration and stays outside the gameplay lists, so old backups import unchanged and a chapter with no authored horizon keeps the layout version it had. What stays in code is what is not a placement: the repeating skyline fields a chapter grows from a seed and a spacing, and the dressing computed from a platform's own width — a cave ledge's crystals exist where a hash of its x says so, and would stop following the deck if they became data. `tests/scene-backdrop.mjs` builds every palette shape on the horizon against the real models and holds the arithmetic to the frame's own numbers.

**The valley's floor has a landmark, and it is the workshop's to move.** A supplied purple canyon arch now stands behind the Sandwright's Pocket — the descent off the plateau ends on the riverbed shelf at x 101-118 and the climb to the summit starts there, so arriving at the violet clay reads as having reached the bottom of the valley rather than another ledge. It stands on that shelf's own top, which layout 12 raised from y .6 to y 3.5, and the arch came up with it. It is set 20 units across, wider than the 17.2-unit mass itself, so the purple runs out from under it, and at depth -16 it belongs to the horizon: behind every cliff block, in front of the nearest parallax band, and out of the shadow pass. What makes it worth having is that it is **not built geometry**: it is the canyon's first authored `decor` placement, so the Decorate panel selects it, drags it, resizes it by either handle and exposes its Size across, Depth, Turn and Lean like any other prop, and **Add → Purple arch** places more. The upload ships at **1.20 MB instead of 17.55 MB** with its mesh copied through byte for byte and hash-verified (`scripts/prepare-purple-arch.py`): its 4096² metallic-roughness map is dropped outright, because the clay material forces metalness to 0 and its own roughness, so the map could not colour a pixel — and a dropped map has to take its texture, image and buffer view with it or the bytes ship anyway. Its orange is harmonised to the chapter's fired clay through the same albedo remap the other five formations use, calibrated to the measured 0.99 red of its own bake; the remap masks on `r>=g>=b`, so the sculpt's violet passes through untouched. Because the route's own `layoutVersion` is unchanged at 11, **no checkpoint is retired and every existing backup still imports** — it gains the chapter's arch rather than losing its scenery. Composition was judged from offline WebGL renders in `docs/canyon-purple-arch/`; live device appearance remains unverified.

**The canyon has a new room before its summit: the Boulder Drop (layout version 12, which retires canyon checkpoints again).** Between the Great Arch's roof and the ropeway the chapter opens up fifty units (`makeRoom` at x270, the way the forest's Weaver's Gap was cut in), and the summit, the ropeway and the bell stand fifty units further on. The cave under the summit's overhang is **floored over with planks** (`plank-floor`, a `break` deck with `timber` and `rockOnly` — a stomp lands on it and leaves it whole), and the only thing in the chapter that goes through them is a **boulder resting in a pool of violet clay on the mesa above**. The way up is the wind's: over a rope bridge to a plateau with a mill, up two cracked ledges to a valve on a perch, back down, and up three ledges on the mesa's flank that no cold jump reaches and the well the valve wakes lifts to. On top the clay is worked the way the pocket's is, except that here it is the ground under the boulder that is raised — it rides the mound up, rolls off it over the hollow's lip, and the chute past the lip does the rest. The boulder is the lab's marble grown up (`marble:{radius:1.5, look:'rock', spill:'right'}` on the station): a marble with an open end named leaves the form for good once its centre is past the brink, with at least `MARBLE.exit` of speed so it clears the cliff, and then belongs to the world — `stepRockFall` in `dist/clay-marble.js` drops it under the player's gravity, bounces it off wall faces, passes it through any `rockOnly` deck it comes down on (which the game breaks with the same `break` event a stomp raises, so the burst, the shake and the sound come free) and rests it on the first floor under that, where it stays: `R` softens the clay but does not bring a spilled rock back, and a save carries spilled rocks (`rocks` in the snapshot) beside its broken decks. A rock station reads "shaped" off the rock, not the clay: it is done — and opens its channel — once the rock is down, seconds after the last stroke, so the routes sweep settles each rock once from the station's solved surface and copies the outcome, and the playthrough pilot stands and watches it go before crossing. The fall is a scene: from the brink to a beat after the rock has come to rest the game watches the rock (`game.cinema`) — the camera pans to it and follows it down through the planks, which fly apart as splinters of the lifts' wood, while the player's hands are off, as they are for the mother's reveal and the dream's pickup; a rock restored where it lay is not watched again. The planks themselves are porous boards in the lifts' wood — pitted like the crumbling ledges, bitten along their edges, gapped between — resting on the pillar with the lifts' twisted rope lashed round their bearers. The canyon's `wall` bodies are now laid in the same kneaded sandstone courses as its decks (`buildCanyonWall`), so the mesa's body, the pillar carrying the planks and the cave's overhang read as rock rather than slabs. The room carries no hint card: the valve, the well, the ledges in its draught and a boulder on a pool of the clay the pocket has taught say what they want. `tests/boulder-drop.mjs` holds the marble's open end, the fall, the flank's dependence on the valve, the planks under a stomp and under the boulder, the authored solve through the real hand, the scene's hold and its end, the way down to the summit and the save; the sections test holds that the drop off the pool lands on whole planks until the boulder is worked.

**The Sandwright's Pocket is wet clay now, and a walk.** The riverbed's mass is the lab's Wet Clay (`pace` on the station: it settles in well under a second, slumps back in seconds, and is held by a hand but not by a rider's weight), resting nearly level and flush with the dock and the landing, so the riverbed is crossed without a thought; its sandstone shelf and the spikes half a unit under the clay's base moved up with it. What the clay is for is a **fourth flower**, on a perch hanging over the middle of the pocket — past a jump off the level clay and past a stomp off it (wet clay throws a stomper less: `launch:14` on the station), within a jump off the pillar a hand pulls up under the jumper, if they go before it melts. The authored solution is that one pull. The pocket's hint card is gone with its old puzzle. The layout audit reads a link off a clay mass at the solved surface under the target when the target stands over the mass, so the perch is measured from the pillar's top rather than the mass's end; the sections test's pocket drill now holds the walk unworked, the perch's two failing approaches and its one succeeding one, the pillar melting under a rider and standing under a hand, R, the beads, the sand and a resume that keeps the flower.

**The canyon has been through the workshop, and the riverbed has a floor (layout version 11).** The sinking run now holds one line of crumbling ledges across the valley with the riverbed **floored in sandstone and sown with spikes** beneath them, so a missed ledge falls somewhere you can see and must not reach — decks carrying a band like that are marked `spiked`, which is how the layout audit tells an authored killing floor from a mistake. The tail of that run stays high and the **pocket is entered from above**: the dock overlooks the clay instead of meeting it, so the clump can be stepped onto. That is not a way through — the pit between its towers is deeper than a hop and the lump's crest is out of reach from the floor of it, so the clay still has to be worked, and a player who drops in works their way out with the same held E that opens the pocket. The room is `rideable` in `tests/clay-sections.mjs` now, like the kiln's tower, and the drill that used to prove nothing there could be jumped proves instead that nothing there carries you across. The gondola swings four units in place of a step, the way into the arch is a crumble and a stub onto a wider bridge rather than two narrow banks, and the arch's flower moved onto the crumbling steps that reach it.

**The Sunbaked Canyon is built around a valley now, and it ends on a ropeway (layout version 10, which retires canyon checkpoints; the other chapters keep theirs).** It used to be one continuous uphill from y 0 to y 22 — windwell taught first, clay pocket at mid-height, bell on the last and highest roof — so the chapter had a gradient but no silhouette. It is the same set of rooms reprofiled: the caravan starts on a **plateau** at y 13 and steps down off it, the sinking sandstone carries that descent to the canyon floor, and the **Sandwright's Pocket sits in the dry riverbed at the bottom** (x92–126, a third of the way along), lowered eight units whole so every relationship inside it is the one it always had — the shelf filling the chasm to 2.15 below the dock, the spikes half a unit under the clay's base, nothing there jumpable. From the riverbed the chapter **climbs for two hundred units** to a summit at y 34.6, half as high again as the old finish, and **wind belongs to that climb alone**: no windwell turns before the riverbed, and the loose tailwind that used to blow over open ground is gone. The Great Arch keeps its cave and its own decks, moved up against the peak, with the rope bridge now crossing *into* the arch's mouth rather than out of it. The deck ids, widths and the pocket's numbers are the ones they always were, so the layout is a diff of heights. **The summit carries a ropeway** — a new `zip` platform kind, the weight-driven family the cave's ferry and the dream's raft already belong to: a trolley on a braided cable between two raked timber masts, which leaves the mast the tick after someone stands on it, eases out and in across sixty units and twenty-four down in four and a half seconds, and hauls itself back up when abandoned, so a missed landing can never strand anyone. It sets the player on a **timber goal deck** — planks, head beam, posts and braces over stacked sandstone (`timber` on an ordinary stone deck) — carrying the finish bell it always had. The ride needed one rule changed: falling more than thirteen units below your last flag kills you, unconditionally, which would have killed the rider halfway down the cable, so that rule now exempts a player a trolley is carrying and grants a short grace after it sets them down. The exemption is the ride's, not the rider's — let go over the chasm and you fall like anyone else. `tests/zipline.mjs` pins the carry, the haul-back, the respawn reset and that boundary; the layout audit gained four ropeway rules (a descent past the 16.8 units a second the landing filter can carry, a cable crossing a spike band, a deck standing level with the ride that would take the rider off it, and a ride ending nowhere to step off), and `scripts/review-canyon-profile.cjs` renders the whole chapter as one strip so the silhouette can be judged as a picture.

**Ember Caverns now reads in layers.** The Turning Heart was redesigned against a supplied target painting and judged blind against it over six rounds: near clay is charcoal-navy, the far field is a paler blue haze of receding spires with dark openings between them, and the ceiling hangs large teeth into the ring. Ledges are sculpted clay bodies with pebbles and moss drips bunched on their lips, the clay-10 wall is dressed with lobes, stalactites and moss curtains as its own streamed scenery view (so its platform view keeps the bounds `tests/walls.mjs` holds it to), crystals sit on real outcrops with a compact cyan halo, mushrooms throw orange pools onto nearby rock, and the orbit ring has rope collars, a Y-rope to its deck and rope-wrapped posts. Collision, routes, machinery and the four-light budget are unchanged. Static clusters bake to one mesh per material and moss uses a coarse retained leaf, so every judged spot draws fewer calls than before (the Turning Heart 528 against 582) for a few per cent more triangles. `scripts/review-level3-visuals.cjs` captures the frames and their cost; `scripts/compare-level3.py` scores a capture against the painting.

**Kneadable clay is no longer a fourth-chapter mechanic, and it is no longer a speed bump either.** Each of the first three chapters stops mid-route, pushes the rest of the chapter bodily to the right and builds a clay section into the space that opens up — `makeRoom()` in `dist/route-authoring.js` does the shifting, so the chapter above each call is still the route exactly as it was authored. The canyon's **Sandwright's Pocket** (x124–160) is one free formable mass — the Clay Lab's `form` rule, promoted — sitting on a sandstone shelf that fills the chasm: it rests as a spire flush with the dock's end, a unit out of a jump's reach and a wall to walk into, a skim of clay over the pit behind it, and a lump against the far cliff cresting above the landing. The shelf is sown with spikes half a unit under the clay's base, so **clay is ground and sand scraped bare is not** — any clay at all is safe, and the mass never thins past a third of a unit between its ends. Nothing there can be jumped; the pocket opens only once the clay is worked, and any shape that carries you counts: the station's authored `solution` — grab the spire's crest and lean it into the pit, grab the lump's crest and slump it into a ramp — is one way of several (`tests/clay-sections.mjs` also crosses it by squashing the spire flat from the air, by pulling a pillar up under your own feet and stomp-launching over the lump, and by E-steps up the lump's face, and once by the keyboard alone). That solution is what "shaped" means for a mass with no finished pose: `solveFormStation` in `dist/clay-rules.js` replays the strokes through the real hand from the clump, the routes sweep copies the surface it makes, the playthrough pilot plays the very same strokes as real pointer inputs, and the tests hold the two to be bitwise equal. The pocket's clay does not slump back on its own (`relax:false`), so what you made is still there when you die and respawn at the dock — R softens the whole pocket back, from off the clay — and its shaped share is stiffer than the lab's (`shaped:.24`), so the toast and the fading hand cue arrive only once about as much clay has moved as the full solution moves, never after a dab or one leaned tower. A checkpoint beyond the pocket resumes with the authored solution rebuilt rather than the player's own heap — the save records that the clay was crossed, not its shape — while a save short of it resumes with the clump, however much was worked, so nothing is ever earned by reloading. A keyboard or a pad opens the pocket too: E works whatever clay is ahead into a step you can walk up — pressing it down where it towers over you, lifting it where it lies below, and at the clay's end, with nothing ahead to step onto, lifting the ground underfoot — and it never draws that clay from under your own feet. The Wildwood's **Weaver's Gap** (x192–232) is two more free formable masses, each on a beam of bark laid across its half of the tear and each sown with thorns just under the clay's base, so the canyon's rule holds here too: a bough stub on the brink, too tall to climb and a wall to walk into, to be leaned out into the bridge across (or built into anything else that carries you), and on the far side a block of clay at the near end of its bark, cresting at the clay's ceiling where no mushroom bounce can land, to be slumped from across the gap into the plate you bounce onto before the drop to the nest. Both keep whatever they are made into (`relax:false`), both have authored solutions the routes sweep and the pilot replay, and the block's near face is the beam's own end, so there is no rounded foot on the approach side to stand on. A keyboard opens the gap too: the canyon's E rule carries the brink to the perch over the bough, and the perch runs up to the mound's bark with the mushroom at its very end, so that from the mushroom's edge the block is within E's reach — held there, the key slumps it until the bounce can land on it (`tests/clay-sections.mjs` walks both by the keyboard alone). Ember Caverns' **Kiln** (x57–93) has a clay tower sealing the way under a rock lintel: stand on it and press, and it squats away from the rock into the kiln floor with you still on it; under the lintel a plug filling the tunnel from floor to rock is spread flat and walked across. Two rules hold for every piece of clay in every chapter, and `tests/clay-sections.mjs` enforces both: **one input works one piece** — no gesture ever moves a second piece of clay — and **clay keeps its volume**, so whatever gets wider gets lower (a ramp counts its wedge, a slab its thickness) within ten per cent — at both ends and at every point in between, because while clay is being worked its height is solved from the blended volume rather than blended on its own, and a half-pressed piece no longer swells to twice its clay; the formable mass keeps its volume exactly, whatever it is dragged into. The Hanging Quarter was brought into line too: its first ramp is pulled from a lump big enough to make it, the laundry wall is one piece pulled over into one slope instead of three slices moving together, and the belfry plug is pressed down into the gap at full depth instead of being squashed thin. For each of the new pieces the same test walks the bypass with only that piece unworked and insists it is impossible, checks the unworked top sits out of reach of the best jump, mushroom bounce or stomp-bounce before it (or is capped by rock — the Kiln tower alone is meant to be climbed onto; for the pocket's mass the top is the first place on its rest surface that is neither a face to slide down nor sand to die on), checks that softening finished clay underfoot opens no way past the next piece, and for the pocket walks a real-input pilot from dock to landing over the solved surface and over the clump — so none of the new clay can be jumped past, and two route links are never asked to vouch for the middle of one mass. Down, out and the free hand's up are taught before the Hanging Quarter; the pull to the right is first asked for there, and both of the Quarter's pulls say so in their own hint. Layout versions move to canyon 9, forest 10, cave 10 and city 6, which retires old checkpoints in all four chapters.

**The Brittle Canopy has a horizon of its own.** Its crumbling boughs used to run against nothing but haze; the supplied waterfall now stands behind them, rising at the right edge as the first bough is reached and standing squarely ahead by the last. It is one authored landmark rather than another tiled crown, so it lives in a parallax layer of its own: a span wider than any chapter means the wrap that keeps the repeating layers full can never bring a second copy into view, and that layer's own drift is what gives the vista a passage-sized life. Its place is read off the passage rather than written as a world coordinate — the passage carries a `backdrop` field in `dist/routes/forest.js`, and both offsets are authored net of the layer's horizontal scroll and vertical follow, so re-cutting the chapter carries the vista along with it. Depth sits it between the two crown layers, where the hazy crowns and the distant ropeways pass behind the cliff while the near trunks still sweep in front of it, and at that distance the fog leaves the rock and the water their colour. It is sunk far enough that the rock runs off the bottom of the frame instead of ending in mid-air. The GLB ships at 0.96 MB instead of 6.52 MB, geometry and UVs copied through unchanged and hash-verified (`scripts/prepare-forest-assets.py`). Composition was matched to the supplied target with live WebGL captures along the passage (`scripts/review-canopy.cjs`, renders in `docs/forest/brittle-canopy/`); scene checks cover the single copy, the depth and drift ordering, that the falls are framed throughout their passage and gone from the rest of the chapter, and that their base stays below the bottom edge from the lowest bough the passage is seen from.

**The workshop can dress a chapter now, not just lay out its route.** A **Decorate** toggle in the tool bar switches the level editor to the chapter's scenery layer, edited with the tools that were already there: tap a prop to select it, drag to move, drag either round handle to resize it about its own centre, and Add offers a palette of shapes rather than platforms. Undo, redo, duplicate, delete, the nudge keys, snapping, Browse and Test all behave as they do for a deck. While it is open the platforms, beads, enemies and hazards stay drawn faintly but cannot be selected, so a tap never has to choose between a prop and the ground behind it, and the sparse foreground props each chapter grows on its own become visible and hold still — for the rest of editing they are hidden, because a prop that fades out under whatever the cursor is over is a prop nobody can position. Each placement carries a shape, a **Size across** that means the width of the silhouette for a built pebble and the supplied castle alike, a **signed depth** (below zero behind the platforms, above zero in front of them), and a **turn** and **lean** in degrees. The palette is per chapter, because a placement names one of the shapes the game already knows how to build: seven — boulders, pebbles, a rock shelf, a clay pot, a torch, a tree and a cloud — take the chapter's own palette and are offered everywhere, while the canyon adds its arches, its purple arch, summits, cacti, tents and cave mouth, the Wildwood its mushrooms, blooms, crowns, groves and falls, Ember Caverns its crystals, amber mushrooms, moss and grotto rock, and the Hanging Quarter its cottages, laundry, doorways and the skyline castle. **The scenery the chapters already carry is editable too, where it was already data.** Everything reachable is outlined, because a landmark stands two to five units above the deck that owns it and a deck's hit line is a thin band at its surface — so the prop you were looking at could be missed entirely while the bare rock below it was the only way in. Decoration mode now draws a dashed box where each prop actually stands, measured against the built geometry rather than guessed at, and a tap inside it selects the platform that carries it; the deck line still wins ties and stays the precise way in. What has no outline has nothing to select, which is how the cacti, moss and checkpoint trees grown from a platform's own geometry are told apart from the landmarks that are real data — and because dressed decks are sparse (eight across the canyon's 365 units), Browse lists every one by its prop and jumps there. Tapping a platform while decorating opens the landmark it carries and its house/arch/entrance/rest flags — and nothing that would change the route: the deck cannot be dragged, resized, nudged, copied or deleted from that panel, and Browse lists every platform already carrying scenery so an authored landmark can be found by name. The same name is labelled as the chapter actually builds it, because the art differs — `arch` is a stone arch in the caverns and the canyon's camp tent, `sandwheel` a windmill everywhere but the canyon, where it is the eroded basin. Two chapters do not let the name decide and the panel says so instead of offering a choice it cannot keep: Ember Caverns keys seven arrangements off the platform's own id, so there the control is a switch that turns the deck's story piece on or off, and the Hanging Quarter does the same for its laundry courtyard while ignoring a landmark entirely on the two decks that tell their story through props. `story-landmarks.js` now reads that id table from the same place the workshop does, so the two cannot drift apart. What is still out of reach is the dressing computed from a platform's own width and position — the moss caps, the tree on each checkpoint deck, the mushrooms, crystals and torches — and the parallax crowns and skylines, all of which would have to become authored data first. **Decoration is scenery and nothing else** — never a collider, a collectible or a checkpoint, and it never reaches the simulation as anything a player can touch, so there is no way to make a chapter unplayable with it; a draft names a shape rather than carrying geometry, and an unrecognised name is refused on import instead of carried. It streams as scenery too, behind every deck the player is about to land on, and backdrop placements drop out of the shadow pass. A chapter with no decoration keeps exactly the layout version it had, so this list existing retires nobody's checkpoints. Checks run at all three levels: the catalogue's shapes, defaults, palettes, carry-with-platform, inertness in the real simulation and refused placements (`tests/editor.mjs`); the toggle, palette, properties, pointer move and handle resize with live preview, history, playtest return and mode boundaries (`tests/editor-ui.mjs`); and every palette shape built in its own chapter against the real models, with exact placement, measured widths, the shadow budget and streaming in and out without disposing a shared model (`tests/scene-decor.mjs`). Live WebGL appearance of a decorated chapter on a device remains unverified.

The world answers as clay too. A landing dents the slab under it: a damped spring with one overshoot, about one and a half per cent under an ordinary landing and three capped under a stomp, scaled down on a long slab because a long plank flexes less. A stomped creature is pressed into a disc like clay under a thumb, and two or three pellets of its own clay squirt out from under the press; the disc is held flat where the player is already looking, gathers itself, and breaks into three to seven chunky clumps of the creature's own colour that tumble out, drop onto the deck below and lie there before sinking away (`dist/clay-shatter.js`). Every stompable creature — clayling, bat, spore puff, echo spitter, dust drifter — dies this way in its own colour; a bat over a pit sheds clumps that simply fall away, a lift carries the clumps lying on it, and the player landing back on the deck presses whatever lies under their feet. It used to squash to nothing in two tenths of a second, which reads as deleted. Neither moves a collider; the dent is skipped under reduced motion, which also keeps a death to two pellets and three clumps dropped gently.

One bench is offered rather than shipped. **The Clay Lab** (chapter picker, under the four) is eleven experiments in what clay does besides hold a shape: a deep block of soft clay that SAGS under your weight and keeps part of the sag, a lump you PACK until it throws you, a row of slabs you SIZE one at a time into a stair, and a whole trough of clay you FORM freely. The fourth, **Fully Formable** (x108–141), has no pose at all: its top is a row of columns over the trough floor (`dist/clay-form.js`, tested on its own and through the real simulation in `tests/clay-form.mjs`), and the pointer is the tool — take hold of any point of the surface and drag, and the clay within a reach follows the hand with a soft falloff, up into a pillar or a step, down into a bowl, sideways into a lean or a bridge; press in from the air above and it dents where the hand enters; tap it and it pokes; hold E and the clay ahead of you is worked into a step you can walk up (pressed down where it towers, lifted where it lies low); stomp it and it craters and throws you straight back up, the way the packed lump does at full but on the first stomp, every time (about six and two-thirds units). Three promises hold every tick: it stays one blob (no column thinner than a third of a unit), it keeps its volume (a pillar digs its own moat and a bowl grows its own rim, nearest first), and it stays usable (no column past 8.5, no face steeper than 4.5 in 1, no corner turned tighter than a 0.6 radius — every edge a stroke would leave sharp is rounded off as it is made — and small strokes smoothed). Weight is a tool here too, lightly and bounded — a stand sinks the boots about a third of a unit and pushes up shoulders, landings and stomps a little more, never past a cap — which is the Sag & Set behaviour inside the one simulation. Collision reads the same columns the mesh does, with two rules a heightfield alone lacks: a face steeper than a walkable slope is a wall — a walker stops at its foot, a jumper who hits it slides down it, and a walker who steps onto one falls — and clay rising into airborne feet lifts them rather than shoving them aside. Left alone (no hand, nobody standing on it) for a few seconds the whole surface slumps slowly back to its authored clump, so a player's ramp or pillar is a temporary solution and the bench is always recoverable; R puts the clump back at once. The slab starts flat and square, a step below the benches, so nothing is shaped for you; the flower perch above is out of a stomp's reach from the slab, so it wants a low pillar pulled up under it and a stomp from its top. Beside it, **A Lump on the Bench** (x141–167) is the same clay sitting free on the bench instead of let into it: a square blob with rounded shoulders whose faces are too steep to climb and whose top is too high to jump to, thinning to nothing at the ends of its footprint (but never past a third of a unit between them, so it is always one lump), with a flower perch so high above it that even a stomp from its top falls short until the lump is drawn up. Lean it into a ramp, spread it flat, pull a step up at its foot or draw it up into a pillar — the bench under it runs on bare on either side. After the lump come **five puzzles and platform stretches built on the formable mass**, each asking one thing of it, so the best of them can be carried into a chapter (`tests/clay-lab-puzzles.mjs` drives every one from its dock through the real simulation, pointer and keys, and holds the same promises every tick as the slab and the lump). **Buried in the Slab** (x166–198): five beads and a flower are inside a deep slab, deepest in the middle; digging is pressing in from the air and dragging down, and because the clay keeps its volume the spoil piles up beside the hole — gathered under the perch it is the mound that reaches it, which nothing on the flat slab does. **Under & Over** (x198–234): the mass lies flush with the dock, a stone lintel crosses it with too little air beneath to walk under (the flower is under it), and past the lintel the bench stands exactly as high as the clay can be raised; a trench under the lintel is the way through and its spoil, leaned into a ramp and raised with E, the way up. **Cast the Mould** (x234–268): a pale outline over the slab is a mould (`mould` knots on the station, legalised like a clump, with the slab derived as the flat that holds exactly the mould's volume); the clay's match to it (`formMatch`, a mean gap read between a cast and a slab) is the station's shaped share and the ribbon's brightness, and a cast latches, announces, and opens the station's `channel` the way a switch would — here a `gate` on a roofed, walled vault with a flower inside, which R shuts again. **Wet Clay** (x268–309): the same mass with a `pace` of its own (`PACE` overrides on the form: it settles in well under a second, slumps in seconds, and — the one real difference — is not held by the weight of a rider, only by a hand), so the step against the far bench melts if you dawdle and the perch is a pillar pulled up under your own feet and a stomp from its top before it sinks. **The Marble Run** (x309–350): a marble (`dist/clay-marble.js`, pure numbers on the same heightfield: it rolls down whatever slope is under it, is carried up when the ground under it is raised, brakes to a stop in a hollow, and bounces off the benches) rests in a hollow at the near end with its socket at the far end past a ridge too tall to roll over; ## Documentation in this repository

This repository holds the game, its tests and the few operational documents needed to run and extend it: this README, `LOCAL-DEPLOYMENT.md`, `EDITOR.md` and `ASSET_REQUESTS.md` (the dimensions and pivots supplied art has to match).

**Design reviews, playtest write-ups, ideation and "what we should do next" documents do not belong in the repository.** They date instantly, they drift out of step with the code, and nothing in the build reads them. Put that thinking in the pull request description, the issue, or the conversation that produced it — the place where it can be answered — and land only the change itself. A previous playtest report and a ten-point design review were removed for this reason; both remain in git history (before commit `491e500`) if they are ever needed again.

`docs/` is therefore not a writing folder. What is left there is evidence and fixtures that code actually uses: `tests/editor.mjs` and `tests/press-relay-fixture.mjs` load JSON from `docs/cave-layout-canon/` and `docs/forest-canopy-canon/`, and the `scripts/review-*.cjs` capture tools write their output into it. Anything else under `docs/` is a leftover capture and can go.

`tests/layout-audit.mjs` is the authoring companion to this rule: run `node tests/layout-audit.mjs [chapter]` while laying out a route and it reports decks buried inside another deck's solid body, hazard bands that reach a standing surface, crossings outside the measured jump envelope, beads inside hazards, flowers with no ledge under them, and enemies crowding a checkpoint flag.

## Chapters

Each chapter is rebuilt around a small set of connected mechanics and is exactly **30% of the previous extended route's distance**. Five or six passages introduce an idea, develop it, change its context, then combine it in a finale. The obsolete district generator is removed. Coordinates, return paths, secrets and safety shelves are authored explicitly.

| Chapter | Distance | Core arc |
|---|---:|---|
| Sunbaked Canyon | 402.5 units | Step down off the plateau on rope lifts; ride the sinking sandstone to the canyon floor; walk the wet clay of the riverbed, or pull it up under yourself and jump for the flower over it before it melts; open a windwell and climb the long wall on wind, lifts and crumbling ledges; cross into the arch and up through its cave; wake the mill's well, ride it up the mesa's flank and raise the clay under the boulder until it rolls off and smashes the planks over the summit's cave; drop through and ride the ropeway down to the bell. |
| Wildwood | 300.5 units | Mushroom chains; stomp into a root cellar; climb both sides of a living tree; open spore vents; cross a brittle canopy, shape a bough into a bridge over the tear in it and slump a block into the plate you bounce onto, then bloom into the crown. |
| Ember Caverns | 324.5 units | Open passages by climbing and returning; ride a clay tower down into the kiln and flatten the plug in its tunnel; steer a ferry through presses; ride rotating cradles; descend to a sunken relay and return by lift; outwit projectile shooters. |
| Hanging Quarter | 300.75 units | Knead a ramp onto the first roof; weigh down beams to raise permanent ropeways; ride awnings and blinking lamp decks; read a shooter from cover; wake the belfry draught, cross failing tiles and press the last bridge yourself. |

Each chapter has secret flowers (three in the canyon, the caverns' five, two in Wildwood and three in the Hanging Quarter), clay beads, checkpoint flags, a finish bell and replay. Thirty-four checkpoints restore health and preserve collectible IDs, elapsed time, broken seals and permanently activated mechanisms. Older layout checkpoints are invalidated so the player cannot resume inside the redesigned terrain; previous best records remain available in the local save history.

`dist/routes/` contains the four layouts. `dist/route-authoring.js` contains only data helpers, never procedural encounters or distance padding. `ASSET_REQUESTS.md` lists optional replacements for the working clay mechanism props.

## Controls

- **A / D** or **left / right arrows**: move.
- **Space / W / up arrow**: jump; hold for a longer jump.
- **S / down arrow**: stomp while airborne; drop through a thin ledge while standing on it.
- **Escape / P**: pause.
- **F**: toggle native fullscreen. Fullscreen buttons are also available in the title screen, HUD and pause menu.
- **Settings → Play fullscreen**: begin directly in fullscreen; touch devices request landscape where supported.
- Touch devices display an analog thumb joystick alongside jump and stomp buttons. A generous neutral zone and gradual speed curve make small steps easier; drag farther for a full run. The joystick captures one thumb until release, even outside its ring, while the other thumb can jump or stomp. Lifting, interrupted touches, pause, fullscreen and rotation reset it safely.

Flower pickups trigger a 0.5-second upper-body flourish while gameplay, the run timer and the current lower-body animation continue. The arms raise a glowing flower, the supplied `dist/assets/flower-victory.wav` plays over ducked music, and the regular follow camera stays unchanged. Reduced motion simplifies the shine. `tests/flower-celebration.mjs` checks concurrent gameplay and cleanup, and `scripts/review-flower.cjs` captures the actual desktop/portrait celebration.

The simulation runs at a fixed 120 Hz and is independent of rendering. It includes coyote time, jump buffering, variable-height jumps, faster reversals, firm braking, a small apex pause, moving-platform carry, short invulnerability after damage and checkpoint recovery. Resize, focus loss and graphics context loss are handled explicitly. Sound uses the Web Audio API after a player gesture.

Static serving requires no build step or external asset services. A modern WebGL 2 browser is required. Serve `dist` over HTTP; opening `index.html` from a file URL will not support JavaScript modules.

Run `npm run check` for movement, causal mechanism behavior, save/restore at all 33 checkpoints, 254 main/optional/recovery trajectories, complete deterministic input playthroughs of all four chapters, controls/fullscreen, animations, assets, clay shaders and streamed scene retention. The checks run across the machine's cores and are found by looking in `tests/`, so a file dropped there runs without being registered anywhere; `npm run check:fast` leaves out the slowest few for use between edits, and `JOBS` caps the workers, which is worth setting when several checkouts are being checked at once. `LEVEL=2 FLOWERS=1 node tests/playthroughs.mjs` also completes the cavern with all three flowers.

Finding a route through a chapter is a search over take-off points, jump holds and waits, and costs minutes; playing one back costs seconds. So a found route is recorded to `tests/fixtures/` and replayed on later runs, which makes the assertion that matters either way: this input stream completes the chapter with enemies, hazards, timers and switches live. The search runs again whenever it could reach a different answer. Each recording carries a hash of the simulation, the chapter layouts and every module those two import, and a recording that no longer plays through is re-searched rather than failed, so a stale or machine-specific one heals itself instead of passing. Changing the simulation therefore re-records the routes, and the rewritten recordings belong in the commit that changed it. `npm run check:full` searches from scratch regardless. Full playthroughs keep enemies, hazards, timers and switches active; a look-ahead input pilot never edits character position or activates channels directly. These checks prove traversability, not human enjoyment or GPU performance. Offline scene exports verify composition with actual meshes and textures; shader expansion is static, not GPU compilation. `npm run dev` serves the static game through Vite; deployment requires no build step.

The available review browser could not create a WebGL context because its graphics support is disabled. Chapter Four was iterated using direct renders of the actual scene meshes to compare the composition with the supplied reference image: camera framing, silhouettes, arches, cloud placement, texture scale, rooftop details and the elevated route. The new ball-derived surface replaces the earlier cube treatment and was reviewed across all four worlds. These offline renders approximate lighting and are not WebGL screenshots; real-device rendering, the background and clay shaders, and performance still require verification on a graphics-enabled device.

The browser reviews screenshot the actual WebGL frame. They share `scripts/support/review.cjs`, which serves `dist` itself on a free port, finds Playwright and Chrome, collects whatever the page complains about, and installs the hook that lets a review drive the running game a frame at a time — so a review runs on its own with nothing started first. `scripts/review-goal.cjs` is the shortest example. The older reviews under `scripts/` still expect a server already running on their own port; the harness is there for when one of them is next needed.

`scripts/export-citadel-scene.mjs OUTPUT_DIR [PLAYER_X PLAYER_Y]` exports the real scene geometry and model textures for an offline composition review. Set `REVIEW_LEVEL=0`, `1` or `2` to review another biome. `scripts/render-citadel-review.py OUTPUT_DIR IMAGE_PATH` renders it with Mitsuba, NumPy and Pillow. Neither review tool is included in the deployed assets or used during gameplay.

All four worlds use overlapping sculpted masses, physically displaced curves and a shared triplanar clay finish on thin platforms, roofs, plants, props and imported models. The original normal maps remain on imported meshes. Rest-space mapping follows animated models and lifts, and accounts for object scale to avoid stretched detail. Only nearby gameplay geometry is instantiated, while background layers wrap to follow the extended routes. Unused clay box geometry is evicted and enemy skeleton textures are released on unload; shared supplied models are retained. Tall terrain uses irregular rounded blocks; continuous caps keep walking surfaces aligned to unchanged gameplay collision. The material uses a shared packed texture and three triplanar samples rather than extra per-frame geometry work. Shared shapes, texture resources and original rigs survive chapter changes. Rebuild the surface maps with `python scripts/prepare-clay-ball.py PATH_TO_CLAY_BALL_GLB` (NumPy and Pillow). The older `prepare-clay.py` is retained as historical tooling and is no longer the production pipeline.

## Custom character

The source mesh, rig, colour texture and all 11 supplied clips are preserved in the GLB. `GLTFLoader` and its dependencies are bundled locally with the same Three.js release as the renderer. The character loads once and is reused across all chapters; loading progress and a retry error are shown before play becomes available.

Gameplay uses the newly supplied `Armature|Idle_9|baselayer` as the standard idle. After four seconds of uninterrupted rest, `Idle_03` plays once as a fidget, then blends back to the standard idle. Movement, airborne states and damage interrupt it and reset the timer; pausing freezes the pose and timer. `Walking`, `Running`, `Regular_Jump`, `Jump_Over_Obstacle_2`, `Face_Punch_Reaction_2`, `Knock_Down` and `Skip_Forward` handle the other states. Walking and running blend with analog movement speed. Jump excerpts cover takeoff, held airborne poses, falling, stomping and landing; moving jumps use the obstacle-leap clip. Damage, checkpoint respawns, pause and chapter completion have explicit transitions. The base pose, pushing and slide clips remain in the asset for later mechanics.

`node scripts/prepare-idle.mjs PATH_TO_IDLE_GLB` verifies compatibility with the existing player rig and generates the small animation-only data file. `node scripts/prepare-enemy.mjs` regenerates the enemy's foot corrections from its shipped GLB. Both preserve the original model geometry, texture and animation poses; translations are corrected so game physics owns movement and feet meet the floor. Enemy walk speed follows patrol speed, pause freezes the animation and a stomp produces a brief squash before hiding the defeated enemy.

The model is uniformly scaled to 1.78 world units, faces the travel direction and has a foot-centred gameplay origin. Root translation cannot move the collision body or double the physical jump height. Grounded clips use offline-sampled sole corrections; no vertex scan occurs in the frame loop. The imported metallic/emissive settings are adjusted to a matte clay surface so the original colour texture responds to canyon sunlight, forest light and cave torches. The supplied topology, texture and bone poses are retained.

`tests/player.mjs` parses the shipped GLB and samples every gameplay clip for finite transforms, stable root placement and floor alignment, then exercises the controller with the real game simulation. Its Node texture hook preserves texture references without uploading images to a GPU. Texture decoding is checked separately; these checks do not establish browser or device rendering performance.

## Choosing a character

Settings holds a hidden cast: the original, the Emberleaf wanderer, the Clay wanderer, the Garden helper and the Clay apprentice. The picker stays out of sight until `ß` is typed with the settings panel open, and once found it stays unlocked on that device. The choice applies immediately — the rig is released and the next one attached on the same group the game already drives, so the title scene or a chapter in progress keeps its position, camera and level. Only the chosen character is downloaded; the others cost nothing until they are picked.

Every character is interchangeable rather than merely loadable. Each carries the same nine states under the same names over the same foot-centred origin, with its own offline-sampled sole corrections, so chapters play identically whoever is worn. Each declares its own standing height — the original 1.78 world units, the three wanderers half again as tall, the apprentice fifteen per cent above the original — and the shadow, the flower reach and the daze motes that circle the head all scale with it. Their rigs answer to one vocabulary of joint names, which is what lets the animation states and the two-handed flower celebration address any of them. Only the original is pulled toward the game's orange by the clay shader; the supplied characters were painted in clay colours already and keep their skin exactly as it was made, taking just the clay surface relief.

The supplied files arrive with at most a single `Running` cycle. `python scripts/prepare-textures.py SOURCE_GLB dist/assets/NAME.glb` repacks their textures to the mobile budget, then `node scripts/prepare-character.mjs NAME` retargets the original character's whole clip set onto the new skeleton and bakes its floor corrections. The original rig rests in an A-pose and the supplied rigs in a T-pose, so what crosses over is each joint's absolute world orientation rather than its rotation away from its own rest — the only constant kept is the roll between the two bone frames once their rest limbs are swung into line. The ankle is the exception: both rigs rest with their soles flat on the floor, and the line from ankle to toe describes the shape of a shoe rather than the direction of a limb, so the foot keeps the alignment of the shin it finishes instead of being aimed. The supplied geometry, skinning and textures are untouched, and the result is written beside the model as `NAME-animation.json` and `NAME-motion.json`.

The three wanderers name their joints the way the original does under a vendor prefix, which is dropped on load. The apprentice came off another exporter with its own anatomy of names, no clips at all, no crown above its head, and its skeleton hanging from an extra root bone carrying the Z-up rotation of whatever authored it — and the game pins a character laterally and drives it vertically through that one hip track, both axis-aligned. `node scripts/prepare-rig.mjs SUPPLIED_GLB NORMALIZED_GLB` settles both before the repack: it renames the joints into the vocabulary the game binds to and composes the root bones away so the hips sit in the model's own frame. Only the GLB's JSON chunk is rewritten — geometry, weights, bind matrices and texture are copied through byte for byte — and both files are then read back and compared joint by joint, so the claim that nothing moved is measured rather than asserted.

`node scripts/review-characters.mjs OUT [STATE] [PHASE] [ID...]` stands the cast side by side in one state on a floor, for an offline look at whether a newly retargeted character carries it the way the others do.

`tests/characters.mjs` loads every character in the registry and holds all of them to the same bar: matching asset hashes, the complete state vocabulary, the declared height over the foot origin, arms that hang the way the original's do measured against the character's own height, feet on the floor throughout every grounded clip, no root drift or airborne displacement, both hands converging on the flower, the declared colour treatment on every material, and walk/run/leap blending under the real simulation. It then swaps through the whole cast inside one running world and back, asserting that exactly one rig is ever attached and that the newcomer takes over the run mid-stride.

The canyon uses blue sky, warm terracotta foreground, peach atmospheric distance, green supplied cacti and orange pulley details. Its supplied enemy/player models and clay surfaces are retained; the shortened route connects windwells, ropes, sinking sandstone and vertical arch paths. Impact feedback includes dust, skids, a brief hit pause, restrained camera shake and collectible feedback. Reduced-motion preferences disable camera shake and hit pauses and reduce particle counts.
